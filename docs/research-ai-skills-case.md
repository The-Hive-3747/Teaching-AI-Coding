# Fundamentals First, Then AI Multiplies Them

The evidence supports the position The Hive already practices: AI-assisted programming is the next abstraction layer in a seventy-year sequence, most layers of which (in the sources found) were dismissed as "not real programming" by the generation before, and every layer of which left the architecture, the specification and the verification in human hands. The labor-market case is strong and specific: a Lehi, Utah employer (Podium) now asks interns for "familiarity with AI-assisted development tools such as Cursor, Claude Code, Codex, GitHub Copilot," Affirm (a US fintech; the posting is for a remote role based in Poland) says "every Affirm engineer builds with tools like Claude Code and most PRs are co-authored with AI," and job ads that ask for AI skills advertise higher pay (**PwC: 62% within occupation, global 2025, no controls for education or experience; Lightcast: 28% overall, US 2024 data published 2025**), though both figures come from vendors and are uncontrolled correlations. The skeptics are also partly right, and the talk should say so: unguarded, answer-giving AI reliably inflates performance during practice and hurts what students can do afterward (a **17% exam-score drop** in the PNAS 2025 field RCT; **50% vs 67%** comprehension in Anthropic's own 2026 study), the harm tracks the *mode* of use (delegation and auto-accept versus questions and reading diffs), and the one well-measured study of experts on their own codebases (METR) found a **19% slowdown**. Those findings do not refute the case; they are the reason for The Hive's 2026 Programming Training Checklist, which forbids AI until a programmer has hand-coded 14 of the 15 chapters of a Java-for-FTC textbook and 40 simulator exercises, because "if you do not understand the basics of programming taught here, you will not be able to debug the code during the season." The Competition Manual's only AI rule (A201) covers portfolios; FIRST's own blog says robot code is allowed with credit; and judges hear from students while adults may only observe silently, so a team that delegates loses the Control Award and a team that abstains loses iteration speed. The Hive's argument, and the structure its checklist already uses, is that the gated position captures both. For the skeptics in the room, the attitude data we have are correlational, but weekly AI users are markedly more optimistic than never-users, so the strongest thing the students can do is let the mechanical mentor who wrote 0 lines of Java demo the robot, and let the fake-gamepad bug show why reading the code is still the job.

## Most abstraction layers drew the same objection, and the architecture stayed human

The history argument is Ben's slide 1, and the research gives it an unusually clean evidentiary spine: the skeptics of most layers are on the record, in their own words, making the same two objections the room will make tonight ("the machine can't do it as well as a human" and "it lets you skip understanding"). One honesty note belongs in the speaker notes: for 1954 the only witness is Backus's own recollection of unnamed skeptics, and the notes found no named 1990s critic of Visual Basic, so "every layer was mocked" is rhetoric; "most layers, in the sources we found" is the defensible version. John Backus, writing the official history of FORTRAN, said **"it is difficult to convey to a reader in the late seventies the strength of the skepticism about 'automatic programming' in general and about its ability to produce efficient programs in particular, as it existed in 1954,"** and his team believed that if compiled code ran "only half as fast as its hand coded counterpart, then acceptance of our system would be in serious danger" ([Backus 1978, HOPL, pp. 166–167](https://softwarepreservation.computerhistory.org/FORTRAN/paper/p165-backus.pdf)). The 1983 satires are the second beat: Ed Post's "Real Programmers use FORTRAN. Quiche Eaters use PASCAL" essay lists as crutches "Source language debuggers... Compilers with array bounds checking" ([Post, Datamation 1983](https://www.ee.torontomu.ca/~elf/hack/realmen.html)), and Ed Nather's "The Story of Mel" insists "Not FORTRAN. Not RATFOR. Not, even, assembly language. Machine Code" ([Nather 1983](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~elb/folklore/mel.html)). Those crutches are now professional hygiene, which is the whole joke the slide should land. For the Java generation the room grew up in, Joel Spolsky wrote in December 2005 that **"Java is not, generally, a hard enough programming language that it can be used to discriminate between great programmers and mediocre programmers"** ([Spolsky 2005](https://www1.udel.edu/CIS/280/tharvey/09S/resources/Joel%20on%20Software%20-%20The%20Perils%20of%20JavaSchools.pdf)), and in the same year Charles Petzold said of IDE autocomplete, almost word for word what people say about AI today, **"And I think it's making us dumber"** and "I don't need to remember anything any more. IntelliSense will remember it for me" ([Petzold, Oct 2005](https://charlespetzold.com/etc/DoesVisualStudioRotTheMind.html)).

The theory that explains why the objection recurs and why it is always half right is Fred Brooks. High-level languages, he wrote in 1987, eliminate "a whole level of complexity that was never inherent in the program at all," but **"the hard part of building software [is] the specification, design, and testing of this conceptual construct, not the labor of representing it,"** and "the hardest single part of building a software system is deciding precisely what to build" ([Brooks, No Silver Bullet, 1987](http://worrydream.com/refs/Brooks-NoSilverBullet.pdf)). That is Ben's sentence, "the abstraction is now the code and the architecture remains," said in 1987. Martin Fowler is the credible modern voice who draws the FORTRAN analogy explicitly: **"the appearance of LLMs will change software development to a similar degree as the change from assembler to the first high-level programming languages"**, and he supplies the honest limit the talk should state rather than let a skeptic raise: "we're not just moving up the abstraction levels, we're moving sideways into non-determinism at the same time," because a FORTRAN function compiled a hundred times "still manifested the exact same bugs" and a prompt does not ([Fowler 2025](https://martinfowler.com/articles/2025-nature-abstraction.html)). Every prior layer was deterministic; this one is not, which is precisely why reading, testing and checking got more important rather than less.

The "you still test, read and check" half of the slide has the best possible primary source: The Hive's own three-day mentor experiment. Across **67 prompts over September 17–19, 2026, with 0 lines of Java typed**, Gemini produced a working TeleOp on the first night; but when the mentors asked for an autonomous, the model took an expedient route and drove the autonomous by simulating gamepad input, because nobody had told it an autonomous was coming (The Hive, docs/verbatim-transcript.md, supplied by Ben 2026-10-03). It worked. Only a human reading the code caught the shortcut. That is Brooks's "testing the fidelity of the representation" and Fowler's non-determinism in one anecdote, and it is also the exact failure the industry's own numbers encode: Sundar Pichai's much-repeated "more than a quarter of all new code at Google is generated by AI" continues **"then reviewed and accepted by engineers"** ([Slashdot, Oct 29 2024](https://developers.slashdot.org/story/24/10/29/2223255/more-than-a-quarter-of-new-code-at-google-is-generated-by-ai); [Fortune](https://www.fortune.com/2024/10/30/googles-code-ai-sundar-pichai)), and Dario Amodei's "90% of the code" retrospective adds that "the teams play an editing and supervisory role" ([OfficeChai, reported Oct 21 2025; conversation date not given](https://officechai.com/ai/my-prediction-of-ai-writing-90-of-code-is-already-true-at-anthropic-anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei/), secondary). Simon Willison's rule gives students a one-line definition of where the line sits: **"If an LLM wrote every line of your code, but you've reviewed, tested, and understood it all, that's not vibe coding in my book—that's using an LLM as a typing assistant"** ([Ars Technica, Mar 2025](https://arstechnica.com/ai/2025/03/is-vibe-coding-with-ai-gnarly-or-reckless-maybe-some-of-both/)), and his follow-up, "AI tools amplify existing expertise," is the thesis of the whole talk ([Willison, Oct 2025](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Oct/7/vibe-engineering/)).

## Skipping AI costs a season of iteration and narrows an already narrow door

Slide 3 has two halves, and the honest version of each is more persuasive than the inflated one. This season's cost is concrete because FIRST permits it and the proof is in The Hive's garage. The 2026–27 BIOBUZZ Competition Manual's only AI rule, A201, says teams "may use AI and research aids to compose their PORTFOLIOS, provided they respect intellectual property rights and include a footnote or endnote credit"; AI appears nowhere else in the Awards section, so the manual is silent on robot code ([FTC Competition Manual 2026–27, Awards](https://ftc-resources.firstinspires.org/ftc/game/manual-06)). FIRST's own blog fills the gap: asked whether teams can use AI, the COO wrote "The answer is yes!", said teams "can use AI to assist in the creation of award submissions, handouts, writing robot code, etc.", that "Proper credit can look like this: Essay created by Team XXXX and ChatGPT, or similar to the byline of this blog post," and that FIRST is "actively encouraging teams to use the power of AI in ways that reflect the team's creativity, effort, and voice" ([FIRST Community blog](https://community.firstinspires.org/expanding-the-first-toolbox-with-artificial-intelligence)). So the competitors a skeptical team meets at the Utah qualifier may already be using it, with FIRST's blessing. What a team gains is iteration: the mentor experiment produced a working TeleOp on night one and a full three-day build in 67 prompts, with the mechanical mentor who does not write Java authoring most of the later changes and eight prompts himself (The Hive, docs/verbatim-transcript.md). The students should be explicit that this half of the slide is an argument built from one adult mentor experiment plus studies of professional developers, not from evidence on students. The professional data say the gain is biggest for people with less time in the job: in the 4,867-developer Copilot field experiment at Microsoft, Accenture and a Fortune 100 firm, developers completed about **26% more tasks per week (SE 10.3%), with short-tenure developers gaining 27–39% versus 8–13% for long-tenure, and junior-ranked developers 21–40% versus 7–16% for senior** ([Cui et al.](https://www.mertdemirer.com/Papers/Demirer_AI_productivity.pdf), partly vendor-authored), and the NBER panel of more than 500,000 GitHub developers finds, for autocomplete tools, gains "from 20% for the most active to 61% for the least active" developers ([NBER w35275](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w35275/w35275.pdf)). Extrapolating from employed professionals to a high-school team goes beyond the studied population; The Hive's read is that a team that is new and part-time sits even further along the "less experienced, less active" axis, which is an inference, not a finding. The same NBER paper is also the reason not to oversell: cumulative gains of **+240% in commits shrink to +30% in actual releases** as output moves up the chain toward something shipped, which for a robotics team translates to "you will write a lot more code; whether it drives the robot still depends on you."

The season cost cuts both ways, and the slide should say so because it strengthens The Hive's position. The Control Award requires a portfolio documenting "hardware/software control components, the challenges each solves, and their functions"; at judging, rules A204 and A208 require at least two student representatives, and an adult may attend but "may not interact or actively coach," so students answer and adults may only observe silently ([FTC Manual 2026–27, Awards 6.3.7, A204, A208](https://ftc-resources.firstinspires.org/ftc/game/manual-06)). Judges hear from students, not the model. A team that delegates its code to AI and cannot explain it loses the award a team that abstains might win; a team that abstains loses the iteration a team that delegates gets. The checklist's own sentence names the trap: "it becomes painfully obvious if you use AI because you cannot answer how your program works" (The Hive, 2026 Programming Training Checklist). The gated position, fundamentals by hand and then AI as a season tool, is The Hive's answer for capturing both.

The future cost is where the students must be most careful, because the strongest evidence is also the skeptics' strongest evidence. Stanford's "Canaries in the Coal Mine" study, the only analysis on this list built on actual payroll records rather than job ads or surveys, finds that **employment of 22-to-25-year-old software developers fell "nearly 20%" from late 2022 to September 2025**, that young workers in the most AI-exposed occupations saw a 16% relative decline, and that the declines occur where AI *automates* work rather than where it *augments* it ([Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, Nov 2025](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2025/11/CanariesintheCoalMine_Nov25.pdf)); the August 2026 dashboard update says the gap "has widened to 19%" ([Canaries Dashboard](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/project/indicators/canaries-dashboard/)). Canaries shows that young workers in AI-exposed occupations lost ground; it does not show that having AI skills protects any individual, and that second half is the team's argument, not the study's. US software-development postings were still about **27.5% below pre-pandemic levels in July 2026**, though up nearly 15% since February 2025 while overall postings fell 7% ([Yahoo Finance quoting Indeed Hiring Lab](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/policy/article/good-news-software-developers-job-postings-are-ticking-up-post-claude-code-154726595.html), secondary), and the recovery is senior-heavy: senior roles were 69.3% of software postings and entry-level 4.5% in Q1 2026 ([hiddenjobs.dev summarizing Indeed](https://hiddenjobs.dev/news/us-software-hiring-recovery-junior-developers/), secondary). None of that is an argument against learning to program. It is an argument that the entry door is narrower than it was, and the evidence on what employers now ask of the people who get through it points two ways that both favor the checklist. First, judgment: PwC finds that "entry-level roles most exposed to AI are now **seven times more likely to require traditionally senior-level 'human-intensive' skills** like leadership, creativity or face-to-face interactions," and that openings for these "seniorised" entry-level roles "have grown 35% since 2019, while other entry-level roles shrank 10%" ([PwC 2026](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-ai-jobs-barometer.html), vendor); that is a finding about human judgment being pushed down to the entry level, which is what the fundamentals-by-hand half of the checklist builds, and it says nothing about AI-tool skills. Second, tool fluency: in NACE's spring 2026 survey of 185 employers (Jobscan-sponsored, fielded Feb 12–Mar 17, 2026), **more than one-third of entry-level jobs require AI skills according to participating employers, and 16.5% of current job descriptions emphasize AI skills, up from 10.5% in fall 2025**; the 16.5% is the harder number ([NACE, spring 2026](https://www.naceweb.org/job-market/trends-and-predictions/demand-for-ai-skills-in-entry-level-jobs-nearly-triples-since-fall-2025)). Full-time job descriptions on Handshake mentioning generative AI have grown almost fivefold since 2023 ([Handshake](https://joinhandshake.com/themes/handshake/dist/assets/downloads/network-trends/class-of-2026-outlook.pdf), vendor). Meanwhile half of graduating seniors are not building AI skills at all (50.5%) and 31% call them "of little or no importance" ([NACE student survey](https://www.naceweb.org/about-us/press/2026/ready-or-reluctant-employer-expectations-for-ai-skills-meet-student-skepticism)), which is the gap The Hive thinks a high-school student who starts now walks through. Kent Beck's line gives the slide its emotional center without a single contested number: **"The value of 90% of my skills just dropped to $0. The leverage for the remaining 10% went up 1000x"** ([Beck, Apr 18 2023](https://tidyfirst.substack.com/p/90-of-my-skills-are-now-worth-0)). The checklist teaches the 10%.

## The Hive gates AI behind 14 chapters and 40 exercises, and the learning science is consistent with that

Slide 4 is the direct answer to "it's a shortcut," and the strongest move is to concede the premise and show the gate. The Hive's 2026 Programming Training Checklist says, verbatim: **"Do not use AI to help you on these assignments. We will probably use AI during the season. However, if you do not understand the basics of programming taught here, you will not be able to debug the code during the season. And, it becomes painfully obvious if you use AI because you cannot answer how your program works."** Before a programmer is cleared, the mentor checks off, in order, 14 of the 15 sections of Alan Smith's *Learn Java for FTC* (hardware and op-modes, variables and data types, gamepad and basic math, making decisions, class members and methods, our first hardware, motors, servos, color and distance sensors, gyro/IMU, dealing with state, arrays, inheritance, rumble; Section 9, analog sensors, is skipped), each "read and code" plus the chapter exercise, typed into the team's own simulator with a physical test harness and Driver Hub ([Learn Java for FTC](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/alan412/LearnJavaForFTC/master/LearnJavaForFTC.pdf)), and then **40 FTCSIM exercises** across Intro (3), Movement (7), Sensors (10), Puzzles (10) and Grabby (10) ([ftcsim.org](https://ftcsim.org)) (The Hive, 2026 Programming Training Checklist). The checklist's stated goal is for programmers to "start the season knowing the basics of Java programming and how to use the libraries to control the robot." That is a lot of hand-coding, with AI explicitly forbidden, before the season; AI enters afterward as a season tool. A coach who hears "The Hive uses AI" and then hears this list learns that the team's programmers do a lot of hand-coding first.

The learning science is consistent with building the checklist this way around, and the students should present the harm studies themselves rather than wait to be ambushed. The cleanest causal study in teenagers is the PNAS 2025 field RCT of about 1,000 high-school math students: vanilla ChatGPT raised practice performance 48%, but on the subsequent unassisted exam those students scored **17% worse than no-AI controls**, while a "GPT Tutor" version prompted to give hints and withhold answers raised practice performance 127% and "largely mitigated" the exam penalty; the authors say students "use GPT-4 as a 'crutch'" ([Bastani et al., PNAS 2025](https://scale.stanford.edu/publications/generative-ai-can-harm-learning)). The ICER 2024 "Widening Gap" study of 21 novice programmers found strong students "were able to use GenAI to create code they already intended to make" while struggling students "finished with an illusion of competence" ([Prather et al., ICER 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17739)). Anthropic's own January 2026 RCT, vendor-authored and not peer-reviewed but notable because it found harm against the publisher's commercial interest, had **52 (mostly junior) software engineers** with at least a year of Python learn an unfamiliar library; the AI-assisted group scored **50% versus 67%** on a comprehension quiz while finishing only about two minutes faster, and the interaction patterns that preserved learning were "conceptual inquiry," "hybrid code-explanation" and "generation-then-comprehension," while "AI delegation" and "iterative AI debugging" hurt ([Anthropic, Jan 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/AI-assistance-coding-skills)). A 2026 preprint of 54 CS students (mostly graduate students, about 5.4 years of coding experience) found agent users finished faster and more accurately but "scored substantially lower in comprehension questions," and that users who reviewed individual file diffs understood more than those who auto-accepted ([Balepur et al., arXiv 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.26375)). Every one of these is the Gemini fake-gamepad story in a lab: the output is fine, the understanding is what is at risk, and reading the diff is what recovers it.

The pro-AI learning evidence is real but narrower, and the room will trust the students more if they size it correctly. The one study of children in the FTC age range, CHI 2023, gave **69 novices aged 10–17 (mean 12.5), with no prior text-based programming**, three weeks of Python with or without Codex; the Codex group had far higher correctness during training (80.1% vs 44.4%) and **no difference on the unassisted post-test a day later (61.3% vs 62.9%)**, with a non-significant edge for Codex a week later, and learners with higher pre-test (Scratch) scores retained more *if* they had used Codex ([Kazemitabaar et al., CHI 2023](https://www.terpconnect.umd.edu/~weintrop/papers/Kazemitabaar_et_al_CHI_2023.pdf)). About 49% of their submitted tasks used AI code unmodified, so the "shortcut" behavior coaches fear occurred and did not measurably damage retention in that short, structured design. Harvard's guardrailed physics tutor roughly doubled learning gains in a 194-student crossover RCT, per press coverage in the Harvard Gazette ([Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2024/09/professor-tailored-ai-tutor-to-physics-course-engagement-doubled/)); a critical review in the ETC Journal questions aspects of the design and should be read before citing it ([ETC Journal, Nov 2025](https://etcjournal.com/2025/11/10/review-of-kestin-et-al-s-june-2025-harvard-study-on-ai-tutoring/)). Read together, what separates the harm studies from the no-harm studies is the **mode of interaction**: delegation and auto-accepting edits versus asking conceptual questions, reading diffs and being made to explain the output. Prior skill is a second, weaker thread: in two studies (Prather, qualitative, N=21; Kazemitabaar) the students with more prior skill got more out of the tool, but Anthropic's and Balepur's participants had years of programming behind them and were still harmed when they delegated, and Kazemitabaar's children had no text-programming background and were not harmed. So the fundamentals-then-AI sequence is not something any cited study tested. It is The Hive's argument, and the structure its checklist already uses: the mode findings say students must read and explain, the prior-skill findings say that is easier for students who already understand the basics, and the Control Award interview makes "explain it" the season's test anyway. The students should say "we think" and "our read," never "studies show," when they make that link.

The official bodies the coaches answer to converge on the same operative rule. The College Board permits generative AI on the AP CSP Create task "as supplementary resources for understanding coding principles, assisting in code development, and debugging," requires the attribution comment "This code was generated using [Generative AI Tool Name]," warns it "can produce incomplete code… code with errors," and holds that **"students must be prepared to explain their code in detail"** ([College Board](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-csp-student-task-directions.pdf)). The Utah State Board of Education's February 2026 framework says **"AI is a tool to augment human judgment, not replace it"** and that students "should not submit AI-generated work as their original work" ([USBE, Feb 2026 draft](https://www.utah.gov/pmn/files/1402559.pdf); its adoption as final is unconfirmed, so call it the state's current framework). CSTA and TeachAI's 2024 guidance argues learning to program matters more, not less, because "people with a greater grasp of underlying computing concepts are able to use AI tools more effectively than those without" ([CSTA](https://csteachers.org/new-guidance-from-teachai-and-csta-emphasizes-computer-science-education-more-important-than-ever-in-an-age-of-ai/)). Thomas Dohmke, then CEO of GitHub (the essay is dated August 3, 2025, and he announced his departure later that month; verify before use), concluded from interviews with 22 heavy AI users that "developers need a deep understanding of programming basics, algorithms, data structures, and overall software systems" to evaluate AI output ([Dohmke & Kalliamvakou, Aug 2025](https://ashtom.github.io/developers-reinvented)). The College Board's rule, FIRST's A201 portfolio credit and the Control Award interview together give any Utah team a ready-made policy: attribute it in a comment, and nobody commits AI-generated code until a student other than the prompter can explain it aloud.

## Hands-on use, not slogans, is the best bet for moving skeptical coaches

The skeptics in a Utah FTC room are not outliers, and the students should treat them as the informed majority rather than as holdouts. In Pew's national teacher survey, **25% of K-12 teachers said AI tools "do more harm than good," rising to about 35% among high-school teachers**, the most negative group ([Pew, May 2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2024/05/15/a-quarter-of-u-s-teachers-say-ai-tools-do-more-harm-than-good-in-k-12-education/)). College Board surveys found **87% of principals worry AI makes students less likely to develop critical thinking** and 89% fear dependence on technology ([EdWeek, Oct 2025](https://www.edweek.org/technology/teachers-worry-ai-will-impede-students-critical-thinking-skills-many-teens-arent-so-sure/2025/10)); RAND finds 61% of parents and 55% of high-school students believe AI harms critical thinking, while more than 80% of students say no teacher explicitly taught them how to use it ([RAND, Sep 2025](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA4180-1.html)). That fear is the one the PNAS and Anthropic studies vindicate for delegation-style use, which means the credible opening is agreement: "You're right that handing a kid ChatGPT and a blank OpMode makes them worse. Here is how we stopped that from happening to us."

The attitude data point toward hands-on use, with a caveat the students should voice. In the Gallup/Walton survey, **48% of teachers who use AI weekly think it will increase student engagement, versus 28% of infrequent users and 25% of those who have never used it** ([Gallup/WFF, Jun 2025](https://www.waltonfamilyfoundation.org/the-ai-dividend-new-survey-shows-ai-is-helping-teachers-reclaim-valuable-time), funder-sponsored). That is a cross-sectional correlation: optimists may simply be the ones who chose to use it weekly, so it does not show that use *moves* attitudes. The Hive reads it as a reason to get coaches hands-on anyway, and a 2024 educator survey's finding that perceived usefulness, not ease of use, drives acceptance points the same way ([Ghimire & Edwards, arXiv 2024](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2406.15360), preprint). The notes found **no study that tests "force multiplier" versus "replacement" framing on educator attitudes**; the augmentation frame is what Utah, FIRST and CSTA already use, and it is a reasonable communication choice, but the students should not claim research says the words work. Translated into a 30-minute kickoff, that produces five practical moves. Lead with the checklist before anyone says "AI," so the guardrails arrive before the objection does. Let Asim, the mechanical mentor who does not write Java, be the one who demos: he authored most of the later changes and sat at the keyboard for eight prompts himself, and a non-programmer getting a robot to move is both the force-multiplier claim made visible and a direct rebuttal to "it's only for kids who can already code." Show the fake-gamepad autonomous as a bug hunt, not a success story, and have a student explain the code the way the Control Award interview requires; that demonstrates the "reading remains" half in a way no quote can. Hand coaches a reproducible policy they can adopt tonight, the College Board attribution comment plus FIRST's credit convention plus the rule that no AI code is committed until a second student can explain it, because the Khanmigo trial shows access without structure produces almost no use (the median student messaged the tutor on a third of practice days) ([Oreopoulos & Low, 2026](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai26-1551), working paper). And close by inviting coaches to try one prompt against a simulator exercise themselves, because getting them hands-on is the team's best bet given what the attitude data show.

## The hostile questions and the honest answers

The talk should not hide the strongest skeptic evidence; it should present it first and show it fits. On **METR**, the facts are these: 16 experienced open-source maintainers working on repositories they had contributed to for about five years, 246 real tasks, Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet in early 2025, and allowing AI **increased completion time by 19%** while the developers believed afterward they had been sped up by 20% ([Becker et al., METR, arXiv Jul 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)). It is independent, randomized and the best-designed negative result available. The answer is not to dispute it but to read its population: these were the world's deepest experts on their own codebases, the group every other study says gains least, and even they could not perceive their own slowdown, which is the strongest argument in the literature for *measuring* instead of vibing, and for the checklist's insistence that students be able to explain what the code does. If a coach says "so it makes you slower," the reply is that earlier field data (2022–23 tools) show short-tenure developers gaining 27–39% ([Cui et al.](https://www.mertdemirer.com/Papers/Demirer_AI_productivity.pdf)) and that the honest range for real-world professional throughput is roughly +10% to +30%, with some experts seeing zero or less; nobody on this team should say "10x."

On the **Stanford Canaries decline**, concede everything: payroll data, nearly 20% fewer 22-to-25-year-old software developers, declines concentrated where AI automates rather than augments ([Canaries](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2025/11/CanariesintheCoalMine_Nov25.pdf)). Then be precise about what it does and does not say: it shows young workers in exposed occupations lost ground, not that AI skills protect any individual. The team's argument from there, labeled as such, is that the jobs disappearing are the ones whose work AI does *instead of* a person, and the roles employers are advertising are the ones where a person directs and verifies it, which is the "reviewer's seat" Affirm is hiring for and the skill the checklist-then-AI sequence is designed to build. On the **PNAS exam drop**, agree that it is the best evidence in the room and note that the same paper's guardrailed condition erased most of the harm; the team's gate is a guardrail with a mentor instead of a system prompt. On **"Anthropic sells the tool,"** the reply is that Anthropic's study found its own product hurt learning, and the team cites it for that. On **"MIT showed brain damage,"** the students should know the "Your Brain on ChatGPT" preprint is about essays, had 18 participants in its key session, and a 2026 commentary says its power analysis implies roughly 159 were needed ([Kosmyna et al.](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/); [Stanković et al., arXiv 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.00856)); do not lean on it in either direction. On **"Harvard CS50 proves it works,"** the honest reply is that it proves students like it, not that they learn more. On **"Amodei said AI writes 90% of code,"** the actual quote has two horizons (90% in "three to six months," "essentially all" in 12) and his retrospective scopes it to Anthropic and its customers, "not uniformly," with humans "in an editing and supervisory role" ([CFR transcript](https://www.cfr.org/event/ceo-speaker-series-dario-amodei-anthropic); [OfficeChai, reported Oct 21 2025](https://officechai.com/ai/my-prediction-of-ai-writing-90-of-code-is-already-true-at-anthropic-anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei/), secondary, conversation date not given). On **"your own robot took a shortcut, so it's unreliable,"** the answer is yes, and that is why the slide before this one says a human reads every diff; Fowler's non-determinism point is the technical version of the same concession. On **"AI code isn't allowed,"** the Competition Manual's only AI rule (A201) covers portfolios and is silent on code, and FIRST's blog says teams can use AI for "writing robot code, etc." with credit. And on **"the Coinbase and Shopify memos are just bullying,"** the students do not have to defend any CEO's management style; the slide claims only that the expectation exists, and the Podium intern posting shows it exists in Lehi.

## Conclusion

The research does something better for this talk than prove the skeptics wrong: it proves them half right in a way that makes The Hive's position easy to defend. The studies that found harm and the studies that found none differ most clearly in how the tool was used, delegation and auto-accept on one side, questions, diffs and explanation on the other; two of them also found that students with more prior skill got more out of it. Nobody has tested the sequence The Hive uses, but the checklist is what those two threads look like when a team turns them into a rule: build the basics by hand, then use the tool in the mode that preserves understanding, with the Control Award interview as the exam. That is a seventy-year pattern too, from Backus's compiler to Petzold's IntelliSense to Fischer's Stack Overflow snippets: each layer removed mechanical work, each was called a shortcut, and each left the person who understood what was underneath more valuable than before. The checklist's two sentences, "Do not use AI to help you on these assignments" and "We will probably use AI during the season," are not a contradiction. They are The Hive's reading of the evidence, and the students should present them that way.

The students should therefore pitch the gate, not the tool. A coach who leaves believing "The Hive makes kids hand-code fourteen chapters and forty exercises before they touch AI, and then a mentor who can't write Java got a robot moving in a night" has absorbed the force-multiplier argument without being asked to accept a slogan, and has been handed a policy they can adopt by Monday. The one thing no slide can supply is the hands-on experience that, in the attitude data, goes with optimism, so the thirty minutes should end with Asim at the keyboard, a student reading the diff aloud, and an invitation to try one prompt on one simulator exercise before the next meeting.
